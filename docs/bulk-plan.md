# The Bench Bulk — Master Plan

**Fran · 190 cm · 86 kg → 95–100 kg · August 2026 → April 2027 · 33 weeks**

Working max used throughout: **bench 115 kg.** Every load below is given as a percentage first and kilos second, so the moment you send real baseline numbers I swap one line and everything recalculates.

---

# Part 1 · How this plan works

## The three rules everything rests on

**1. Buy strength and size separately.**
Strength comes from heavy work at low volume. Size comes from submaximal work at high volume. Trying to buy both from the same sets is what maxing every session was — and it's why the bench, the most volume-dependent of the big lifts, is the one that punished it hardest.

**2. Fatigue tracks how close you go to failure, not how many sets you do.**
Six sets at 3 reps in reserve recover as fast as three sets at 3 RIR. Three sets to failure cost you 24–48 hours. This is why you can bench four times a week — almost nothing goes to failure on the bench.

**3. Failure isn't cut, it's aimed.**
It goes on cables, machines and isolation work, where it's the actual mechanism of growth and costs nothing systemically. It never goes on heavy pressing, where the strength data says it buys nothing.

## The rotation

Six sessions, run in order. You train when you can. You always just do the next one.

**A → B → C → D → E → F → A → B → …**

| | Session | Primary job |
|---|---|---|
| **A** | Bench HEAVY + Back | Max-strength specificity |
| **B** | Lower + Arms | Leg hypertrophy, arm volume |
| **C** | Bench VOLUME + Upper Chest | The main hypertrophy driver |
| **D** | Speed Bench + Overhead + Back | Skill practice at near-zero cost |
| **E** | Bench VARIATION + **AMRAP** | Supplemental strength + your weekly measurement |
| **F** | Lower + Delts + Jumps | Legs, athleticism, side delts |

**Why this order.** Zourdos et al. tested the same three sessions in two orders and found a statistically significant bench advantage for the order that puts the *low-fatigue* session between the volume session and the heavy session. That's built in here: after the volume day (C) comes the speed day (D), not the AMRAP. And the heavy day (A) always sits two sessions after the AMRAP.

Never reorder so that A follows C or E directly.

## Where to put your rest days

The rotation doesn't care about calendar days, but fatigue does. Best places to pause:

| Pause here | Why |
|---|---|
| **After A** | The heaviest neural load of the rotation |
| **After E** | The only true-failure compound work you do |
| **After F** | Natural cycle boundary, and jumps are more taxing than they feel |

**Worst place to pause: between C and D.** The speed day is deliberately cheap and exists partly to flush the volume day — skipping past it wastes the design.

If you train 6 days in 7, take the single rest day after A or after E. If you train 5, rest after A *and* after E. If you're doing three in a row, make it B → C → D (only one of those is fatiguing).

# Part 2 · The six sessions

Failure notation: **[F]** means take it to genuine failure. Everything unmarked stops at the stated RIR.

## SESSION A — Bench Heavy + Back

**What it's for:** this is the session that makes 140 kg feel normal. Heavy singles teach your nervous system to produce force at loads close to your max, and they teach your body the *skill* of the lift at competition weight. The back-off sets are what actually make you stronger — the single on its own barely works.

> **The evidence for that last sentence:** a controlled trial ran heavy-single-alone against heavy-single-plus-back-offs for six weeks. Single alone: +11.4 kg total, with a 6.3% probability of a meaningful change. Single plus 2×3 at 80% of it: **+33.7 kg, 99.6% probability.** The single is a load-finder. The back-offs are the training.

| # | Exercise | Sets × Reps | Load | Effort |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Comp bench (paused)** — top single | 1×1 | RPE 8 ≈ 92% ≈ **105 kg** | RPE 8, hard cap 8.5 |
| 2 | **Comp bench** — back-offs | 3×3 | 80–85% of your single ≈ **85–90 kg** | 2–3 RIR |
| 3 | Weighted pull-up *(tracked grip)* | 4×4–6 | add weight | 2 RIR |
| 4 | Chest-supported or cable row | 3×8–12 | — | 1–2 RIR |
| 5 | Cable lateral raise | 4×12–20 | — | last 2 sets **[F]** |
| 6 | Overhead cable triceps extension | 3×10–15 | — | last set **[F]** |
| 7 | Reverse hyperextension | 2×12–15 | — | 3 RIR |
| 8 | Wrist curl + reverse wrist curl | 2×12–20 each | — | **[F]** |

**The heavy single rules — read these every time until they're automatic:**

1. **Write the load range down before you start.** Pre-commit. This is the whole ballgame.
2. **Maximum two singles above 90%.** Then stop, regardless of how good they felt.
3. **Never miss a rep.** A miss means the ceiling failed.
4. **If you had to psych up, it was too heavy.** No slaps, no ammonia, no music change. RPE 8 should feel businesslike.
5. **Back-offs are not optional.** Skipping them turns this into the thing that failed you before.

**What it's for:** most of your leg hypertrophy, and a big chunk of your arm volume, in a session that costs your bench nothing. This is where training to failure earns its keep — everything here is a machine or a cable, so failing costs you the rep and nothing else.

## SESSION C — Bench Volume + Upper Chest

**What it's for:** the biggest single contributor to chest growth in the rotation, and — because size is what eventually moves the bench — a major contributor to the 140 kg. This is also your densest upper-chest session, which is one of your named aesthetic priorities.

| # | Exercise | Sets × Reps | Load | Effort |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Comp bench (paused)** | 5×5 | 75–80% ≈ **86–92 kg** | 2–3 RIR |
| 2 | Incline press — 30°, DB or Smith *(one per block)* | 3×8–12 | — | 1–2 RIR |
| 3 | Incline cable fly | 3×12–15 | deep stretch, squeeze at the top | all **[F]** |
| 4 | Lat pulldown, wide grip | 3×8–12 | — | 1 RIR |
| 5 | Cable lateral raise — cable behind the body | 4×15–20 | — | all **[F]** |
| 6 | Rear delt fly or face pull | 3×15–20 | — | **[F]** |

**Why 30° and not 45°:** the lower incline keeps more tension on the clavicular pec and less on the front delt. Above about 45° the exercise quietly becomes a shoulder press.

**Why the cable is behind your body on the laterals:** it loads the side delt in its lengthened position, where the evidence for growth is strongest. Cable and dumbbell laterals were found equivalent in a 2025 within-participant study, so use whichever lets you reach genuine failure without swinging — but the behind-the-body cable position is the upgrade.

## SESSION D — Speed Bench + Overhead + Back

**What it's for:** a fourth bench exposure that costs almost nothing, plus your overhead press progression, plus heavy back work. Non-failure sets at this effort recover in about six hours, so this session is essentially free frequency.

**And it's where the biggest free win in the plan lives.** A study equated volume and load exactly across six weeks and varied only the *intent* to move the bar fast: maximal intent produced **+18.2% on the bench**, deliberately slow produced **+9.7%**. Double the gain from identical work.

The catch: that effect only exists when sets aren't taken to failure. Which is another reason failure stays off the bench.

| # | Exercise | Sets × Reps | Load | Effort |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Speed bench (paused)** | 6×3 | 60–70% ≈ **70–80 kg** | RPE 5–6 — must stay snappy |
| 2 | **Overhead ladder** *(see Part 6)* | 3×8–12 | — | 2 RIR |
| 3 | Weighted chin-up or pull-up *(second grip)* | 3×6–10 | — | 1–2 RIR |
| 4 | Cable row — different angle to Session A | 3×10–15 | — | 1 RIR |
| 5 | Cable lateral raise | 3×12–20 | — | last 2 **[F]** |
| 6 | JM press or pushdown | 3×10–15 | — | last set **[F]** |
| 7 | Cable or preacher curl | 3×8–12 | — | last 2 **[F]** |
| 8 | Wrist roller or heavy holds | 2 sets | — | **[F]** |

**Do not let the speed day get heavy.** If the bar isn't visibly faster than your work sets on C, the load is wrong. The single most common way people ruin a heavy/light structure is by making the light day hard.

## SESSION E — Bench Variation + AMRAP + Arms

**What it's for:** supplemental pressing strength through a different stimulus, plus the one measurement each week that tells us objectively whether any of this is working.

| # | Exercise | Sets × Reps | Load | Effort |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **THE AMRAP — comp bench, paused** | 1 × max reps | 80–87% ≈ **92–100 kg** | **[F]** — spotted, **done fresh** |
| 2 | **Bench variation** *(rotates by block)* | 4×4 | ~72–78% of comp max ≈ **83–90 kg** | RPE 7–8 |
| 3 | Weighted dip — slight forward lean | 3×6–10 | — | 1–2 RIR |
| 4 | T-bar or chest-supported row | 3×8–12 | — | 1 RIR |
| 5 | Cable lateral raise | 4×12–20 | — | last 2 **[F]** |
| 6 | EZ-bar or cable curl | 3×8–12 | — | last 2 **[F]** |
| 7 | Forearm work — your choice | 2 sets | — | **[F]** |

### The AMRAP protocol

This is the most important 90 seconds of your week. Do it right:

- **Same load family every time within a block.** Pick a load in the 80–87% window and keep it. Comparability is the entire point.
- **Same bench standard.** Paused, roughly one second, moderate arch. A touch-and-go rep does not go in the log next to a paused one.
- **Get a spotter.** Physically standing there, not "nearby." No spotter available means the AMRAP moves to a machine or dumbbell press that day.
- **Log the load, the reps and the RPE.** The app converts it to an estimated max.
- **Never do more than one per week.** A compound set to failure costs 24–48 hours of recovery. Two per week and each measurement is taken against incomplete recovery from the last one, which makes the whole series meaningless.

**Why this load range and not something lighter:** at 80–90% the rep count agrees between sessions to within about one rep, and the formula error at ≤5 reps is near zero. At 70%, the between-person spread runs from 6 to 28 reps and the error roughly doubles. A moderate-rep AMRAP feels productive and tells you nothing.

And if your back objects at all, they come out and we revisit later.

# Part 4 · The 33 weeks

| Block | Weeks | Theme | Bench variation | Top-set cap |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **0 — Re-entry** | 1–3 | Rebuild capacity, groove technique, set baselines | Close-grip | **RPE 7.5, no singles** |
| **1 — Accumulation** | 4–9 | Volume emphasis at moderate load | Close-grip | RPE 8 |
| **2 — Intensification** | 10–15 | Heavy singles proper. **Static holds introduced.** | Spoto press | RPE 8.5 |
| **3 — Accumulation II** | 16–21 | Volume again, at higher absolute loads | Pin press / dead bench | RPE 8 |
| **4 — Intensification II** | 22–27 | Heaviest sustained block | Close-grip, heavier | RPE 8.5 |
| **5 — Specialisation** | 28–31 | Bench volume up, accessories trimmed | Spoto press | RPE 9 |
| **Peak** | 32–33 | Taper and test | — | RPE 10 once |

**Week 6 of every block is a deload.** Keep all six sessions, cut volume 40–50%, drop top-end intensity about 10%, everything at 3+ RIR minimum. **Never take a full rest week** — a controlled trial found complete cessation actually *impaired* strength relative to training through, and the cessation group reported more soreness and less motivation, not less.

**Variations rotate on block boundaries, never weekly.** A systematic review found that excessive rotation frequency may actively hinder adaptations. Six weeks on one variation, then switch.

## How loads progress

- **Comp bench top single (A):** work up by feel to the capped RPE. It rises on its own as you get stronger. Don't force it.
- **Comp bench volume (C):** add 2.5 kg when all 5 sets hit the top of the rep range at the prescribed RIR.
- **AMRAP (E):** load is fixed within a block. Progress shows up as *reps*.
- **Accessories:** double progression. Hit the top of the range on all sets, then add weight and drop back to the bottom.

**Do not ramp weekly volume.** Two randomised trials tested progressively increasing weekly sets against holding volume constant and found no advantage. Pick a sustainable volume, hold it, progress load and quality instead.

| | Contents |
|---|---|
| **1** | Bench top single + 3×3 back-offs, weighted pull-up, laterals, triceps |
| **2** | Leg press, leg curl, quad extension, calves, curls |

# Part 6 · The two progressions

## The overhead press ladder

Your shoulder pain came at the very start of your training, on a lift you haven't touched since. That's almost certainly a preparedness and technique problem, not a structural one. So we rebuild the position rather than just adding the lift back.

**Four weeks minimum per rung. Advance only if the previous rung was completely symptom-free.**

| Rung | Exercise | Weeks |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Seated neutral-grip dumbbell press | 1–6 |
| 2 | Landmine press | 7–12 |
| 3 | High-incline press (~60°) | 13–18 |
| 4 | Seated barbell press | 19–24 |
| 5 | Standing barbell overhead press | 25+ |

**Drop back a rung at the first sign of dull ache.** No exceptions, no negotiating. You have 33 weeks; there is no rush.

Two things to watch as you climb: you need roughly 170° of shoulder flexion with good thoracic extension to press overhead safely. If you can't get there, the body compensates by hyperextending the lumbar spine — which, given your back, is the second thing we don't want. Work on thoracic extension and lat length alongside the ladder.

## Static holds — from block 2 (week 10)

You mentioned these helped when you were at your best. They probably did, for three reasons: they desensitise you to heavy loads, they build lockout and connective tissue, and they can potentiate the sets that follow.

But they're **joint-angle specific** — a lockout hold trains your lockout. At 190 cm with long arms, your sticking point is almost certainly mid-range, so holds are a load-exposure and confidence tool, not a fix for your actual weak point.

**Protocol, once per week on Session A, after the main work:**

- 2–3 holds of 5–10 seconds at **105–115% of current 1RM** (start at ~120 kg)
- Pair with **pin presses 5–10 cm off the chest** — that's where the transfer actually lives
- **Rack pins set, spotter present, always**
- Heavy emphasis in the final peaking block — this is where they shine

Not before week 10. You've come off a cut, and hanging 115% of your max on your elbows before the connective tissue has re-adapted is how you buy a six-week layoff.

# Part 7 · Exercise technique

## Bench press — the lift that matters

**Setup, in order, every single time:**

1. Eyes under the bar. Feet planted, moderate arch **from your upper back, not your lower back**.
2. Grip: ring fingers on the rings — that's your current setting, about 73 cm, and we'll test alternatives.
3. Pull your shoulder blades back and *down*. Not just back — down. Think about putting them in your back pockets.
4. Unrack. **Then pause 2–3 seconds** to re-settle the arch, re-plant the feet, re-tighten the lats. This is the cue that powerlifter gave you and it's the best free advice in this document.
5. Big breath, hold it.

**The rep:**

- Control the descent — **roughly 2 seconds**. This is your biggest technical leak. Rushing means an inconsistent touch point, a variable bar path, and a bounce that inflates your numbers without building the strength underneath.
- Touch the same spot every rep. Lower chest, just below the nipple line.
- **Pause on the chest** — about one second, bar dead, still tight.
- Then drive with **maximal intent**. This is the free 8% from the intent research.
- Bar path is a **J-curve**: back and up off the chest, then vertical once the bar is over your shoulder joint. Not straight up.
- Leg drive: push your feet into the floor and *toward your head*, not up. Nothing should lift off the bench.

**Your specific leaks, in priority order:**
1. Rushing sets → slow the eccentric, pause after the unrack
2. Long arms mean a long range → grip width and bar path matter more for you than for most people
3. Moderate arch is the *right* call given your back — build range through thoracic extension and hip flexors, never through lumbar hyperextension

## Everything else

| Exercise | The cues that matter |
|---|---|
| **Incline press (30°)** | Same scapular setup as flat. Full stretch at the bottom — this is where the growth is. Don't lock out hard; keep tension on the pec. |
| **Incline cable fly** | Slight elbow bend, fixed throughout. Think about the stretch, not the squeeze. Let the arms travel *behind* the torso at the bottom. |
| **Weighted pull-up** | Start from a dead hang with shoulders depressed. Drive elbows down and back, not just up. Chest toward the bar. Control the descent. |
| **Weighted dip** | Slight forward lean for chest bias, upright for triceps. **Stop the descent when your upper arm is parallel** — deeper is where shoulders get hurt for no extra benefit. |
| **Cable lateral raise** | Cable **behind your body**, so tension exists in the stretched position. Lead with the elbow. Slight forward lean. Stop at shoulder height — above that the traps take over. Slow negative. |
| **Cable row** | Chest up, no torso swing. Pull to the lower ribs. Let the shoulder blade protract fully at the front for the stretch. |
| **Overhead triceps extension** | The only movement that meaningfully loads the triceps **long head** — bench pressing barely touches it. Elbows in and pointed up, full stretch behind the head. |
| **Pushdown** | Elbows pinned to your sides. No shoulder movement. Squeeze hard at the bottom. |
| **Incline dumbbell curl** | Bench at 45°, arms hanging **behind** the torso. This is the long-head stretch position — the reason to do them on an incline at all. |
| **Leg press** | Feet mid-to-high. **Stop before your pelvis tucks.** Control the descent. Don't lock the knees out hard at the top. |
| **Seated leg curl** | Seated beats lying — hip flexion puts the hamstring at a longer length. Control the return; don't let the weight snap back. |
| **Quad extension** | Pause at full extension. Slow negative. This one is genuinely good to failure. |
| **Calf raise** | 2-second pause at full stretch. Full range. Rushing calf work is why calves don't grow. |
| **Reverse hyper** | Controlled, no swinging. This is a decompression and blood-flow exercise for your back, not a strength lift. Stop at neutral, don't hyperextend. |
| **Wrist curls** | Full range, forearms braced on a bench. High reps, always to failure. |

# Part 8 · Exercise variation — your question answered

You asked whether to pick the variation that suits you or rotate weekly. The evidence has a clear answer.

**Don't rotate weekly.** A systematic review found that excessive rotation — high frequency of change — may hinder adaptations. And a direct trial comparing fixed exercises against randomly varied ones found the fixed group gained **+4.7% on bench** versus **+0.77%** for the varied group. (The varied group did enjoy training more, which is why the accessories rotate at all.)

**The rule: anchor what you measure, rotate what you don't.**

| Category | Rotation |
|---|---|
| Comp bench | **Never.** It's the skill you're building. |
| Bench variation slot | Every 6 weeks, on block boundaries |
| Tracked pull-up, tracked incline | Fixed for a whole block |
| Untracked accessories — rows, curls, laterals | Every 6 weeks, or whenever stale |

### Your specific choices

**Pull-up grips.** You like all three, and they *are* meaningfully different — chin-ups bias biceps, wide grip biases lats in the frontal plane, neutral is the friendliest on the elbow.

Do this: **pick one as the tracked weighted lift and keep it for the block.** I'd suggest **neutral or normal pronated** — chin-ups get limited by your biceps before your back, which makes them a worse strength lift.

The other grips still appear, as the unweighted second exposure on Session D. So you get the variety and you keep a clean progression line.

**Incline: dumbbell or Smith.** Both are good, for different reasons.

- **Dumbbells** give a longer stretch at the bottom, and training at long muscle lengths shows roughly 6–15% more growth in comparisons.
- **Smith** lets you load heavier and progress more precisely, and there's no getting-the-dumbbells-into-position tax at heavy weights.

**Use one per block.** Dumbbells in accumulation blocks (1 and 3), Smith in intensification blocks (2 and 4). You get both benefits and one clean progression line at a time.

**Rows: cables are fine.** You like them, they're joint-friendly, and the loading curve is good. Just vary the *angle* between Session A and Session D — one higher toward the upper chest, one lower toward the ribs — rather than swapping the machine.

**The general principle:** if you're tracking it and trying to add weight, keep it fixed. If it's there for volume and you're bored, change it. Two versions of the same thing swapped weekly is the worst of both worlds — you never accumulate a progression on either.

# Part 9 · Nutrition

You don't track calories and you have a large appetite. So this is built around **outcomes, not arithmetic.**

## The targets

| | Target |
|---|---|
| **Weeks 1–12** | +0.4–0.5 kg/week |
| **Weeks 13–33** | +0.2–0.25 kg/week |
| **Protein** | 1.6–2.2 g/kg → **150–200 g/day** |
| **Creatine** | 3–5 g/day, monohydrate, any time, no loading |

**Why faster early:** you're regaining tissue you already had, and muscle memory means the first 8–12 weeks convert food into muscle at a much better ratio than the rest of the bulk will. Push while that's true, throttle back once you're past your old peak.

## How to steer without counting

You need two instruments and one rule.

- **Scale:** every morning, after the toilet, before food. **Only the 7-day average counts.**
- **Tape measure:** waist at the navel, once a week, relaxed, same time of day.

**The rule:** adjust on 2–3 week trends, never on single readings.

| What you see | What to do |
|---|---|
| Gaining under 0.1 kg/week | Add ~250 kcal — realistically, one extra meal-sized snack |
| Gaining over 0.6 kg/week | Cut ~300 kcal |
| Waist rising nearly as fast as bodyweight | Slow the bulk regardless of what the scale says |
| Weight up, waist flat, lifts up | Do absolutely nothing. This is what it's supposed to look like. |

## Your foods

Greek yoghurt, bananas, chicken and rice are a genuinely good base — you've picked well. Around those:

**Protein anchors** — hit these and the target takes care of itself: Greek yoghurt · chicken · eggs · milk · quark/cottage cheese · whey · lean beef · fish

**Carb anchors:** rice · potatoes · oats · bread · pasta · bananas and other fruit

**Fats:** olive oil · nuts · whole eggs · fatty fish

**The one thing worth actually counting: protein.** Everything else can be eyeballed on a bulk, because the scale tells you whether total intake is right. Protein is the only nutrient where you can't see the deficit until months of training have been wasted. Count it for two weeks until you know roughly what 180 g looks like on your plate, then stop counting.

**A practical trick for untracked meals:** don't try to estimate them. Instead, make sure your *tracked* portions — breakfast, post-workout, before bed — reliably deliver 120–140 g of protein. Then whatever the untracked meals add is a bonus rather than a gap.

## Carbs — when it actually matters

Honestly? On a surplus with a big appetite, you'll be well-fuelled almost all the time and this barely matters. But where it does:

| When | What |
|---|---|
| **Before Session A (heavy) and Session E (AMRAP)** | A carb-based meal 2–3 hours before. These are the two sessions where being under-fuelled visibly costs you performance — and E is a *measurement*, so a bad reading corrupts data, not just a workout. |
| **Before Session C (volume)** | Same. Highest total work of the rotation. |
| **Before D and F** | Doesn't matter much. Train fasted if you like. |
| **Around a deload** | Don't cut food. The deload is for recovering, and recovery costs energy. |
| **If a session feels flat and it isn't sleep** | It's almost always carbs the day before, not carbs that morning. Glycogen fills over about 24 hours. |

**Don't do anything clever.** No carb cycling, no timing protocols, no fasting windows. On a bulk with your appetite, "eat enough and eat protein" captures 95% of the available benefit.

# Part 10 · Cardio, steps and basketball

You already walk a lot and play basketball occasionally. That's a good setup and I'm not adding structured cardio to the split.

**Keep the walking.** It burns energy without meaningfully interfering with lifting, it helps you eat more without gaining fat as fast, and it aids recovery. If anything, treat your step count as a dial: if the waist is creeping, add steps before you cut food.

**Basketball — where to put it:**

| Timing | Verdict |
|---|---|

| After Session B or F (leg days already done) | ✅ Fine |

# Part 13 · Operating manual

Everything in this section is about *how* to execute, not *what* to execute.

## 13.1 Notation — what the prescriptions actually mean

| Written | Means |
|---|---|
| `4×12–20, last 2 [F]` | Four sets. Sets 1–2 stop at **1–2 RIR**. Sets 3–4 go to genuine failure. |
| `3×10–15, last set [F]` | Sets 1–2 at 1–2 RIR, set 3 to failure. |
| `3×12–20, all [F]` | Every set to failure. Only ever on isolation and machines. |
| `5×5, 2–3 RIR` | Every set stops 2–3 reps short. No set goes to failure. |
| `1×1 @ RPE 8` | One single, at a load you could have done two more reps with. |

**The rule for unmarked sets inside a `[F]` exercise: 1–2 RIR.**

They're not junk sets — they're a ramp. Taking set 1 of 4 to failure costs you roughly 30% of the reps on the sets that follow, which means you trade a small amount of extra stimulus on one set for a large loss of volume across three. Ramp in, then finish hard.

## 13.2 RPE, RIR, and percentages

**RPE and RIR are the same scale from opposite ends.** RPE 8 = 2 reps in reserve. RPE 9 = 1 RIR. RPE 10 = failure.

### The conversion table (Tuchscherer / Helms)

Percentage of your 1RM, by reps and RPE:

| Reps | RPE 10 | RPE 9.5 | RPE 9 | RPE 8.5 | **RPE 8** | RPE 7.5 | RPE 7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | 100 | 97.8 | 95.5 | 93.9 | **92.2** | 90.7 | 89.2 |
| **2** | 95.5 | 93.9 | 92.2 | 90.7 | **89.2** | 87.8 | 86.3 |
| **3** | 92.2 | 90.7 | 89.2 | 87.8 | **86.3** | 85.0 | 83.7 |
| **4** | 89.2 | 87.8 | 86.3 | 85.0 | **83.7** | 82.4 | 81.1 |
| **5** | 86.3 | 85.0 | 83.7 | 82.4 | **81.1** | 79.9 | 78.6 |
| **6** | 83.7 | 82.4 | 81.1 | 79.9 | **78.6** | 77.4 | 76.2 |
| **8** | 78.6 | 77.4 | 76.2 | 75.1 | **73.9** | 72.3 | 70.7 |
| **10** | 73.9 | 72.3 | 70.7 | 69.4 | **68.0** | 66.7 | 65.3 |
| **12** | 68.0 | 66.7 | 65.3 | 64.0 | **62.6** | 61.3 | 59.9 |

So: 6 reps at RPE 8 = **78.6%** of your max. At a 115 kg bench, that's 90 kg.

### Where percentages apply — and where they don't

**Use percentages for:** competition bench, bench variations, weighted pull-up, weighted dip, incline press. Anything where you have a real max and you're working at 1–6 reps.

**Do NOT use percentages for:** lateral raises, curls, pushdowns, flies, leg curls, quad extensions, calves. Three reasons:

1. **RIR accuracy collapses at high reps.** At around 5 reps the average error is 0.65 reps. At around 16 reps it's **5.15 reps** — the estimate becomes meaningless.
2. **Reps at a given percentage vary enormously.** At 70% of 1RM, trained lifters range from **6 to 28 reps**. Different exercises need entirely different tables — one study had to build separate ones for bench and leg press.
3. **You'd need a 1RM for every exercise**, which means testing a one-rep-max lateral raise. Absurd.

**For accessories, the best predictor of today's load is last session's load.** Not a percentage of anything. The app should prescribe from your own history, which is more accurate than any formula.

## 13.3 Should you use feel or calculation?

**Both, and the gap between them is information.**

- The app calculates a **starting** load from your stored max.
- You execute the set and record what **actually** happened — reps and RIR by feel.
- If the gap is consistent, your max is wrong, and the app tells you.

You will most likely **undershoot** — research finds people underpredict reps to failure by about one rep on average, with big individual variation, and training history doesn't improve this. So your "2 RIR" is probably 3 RIR. That's the specific reason for the weekly calibration set: pick an isolation exercise, call your RPE out loud mid-set, then go to true failure and count the difference.

## 13.4 Updating your maxes — the rule that prevents a slow disaster

Two separate numbers, and confusing them is how people overreach:

| Number | What it is | How often it changes |
|---|---|---|
| **Observed e1RM** | Computed from every qualifying set. A continuous record. | Every session |
| **Working max** | The number prescriptions are calculated from. | **Once per block** |

**Why the working max updates only at block boundaries:** the test–retest variability of a 1RM in trained lifters is about 3.3%, which on a 130 kg bench is ±4 kg of pure noise. If you bump your working max every time you have a good day, the loads ratchet upward on noise, and six weeks later you're training at genuine RPE 9.5 while believing you're at 8. That is overreaching by arithmetic, and it's very hard to notice from inside.

**The rule:**

- At the end of each block, set the working max to the **best observed e1RM of that block** — from your heavy singles or your AMRAPs, whichever is higher.
- Mid-block exception: if observed e1RM exceeds the working max by **more than 5% for two consecutive weeks**, bump it early.
- Never lower it mid-block. A bad week is a bad week, not a strength loss.

**Which lifts get a stored max at all:** competition bench, bench variations, incline press, weighted pull-up, weighted dip, leg press. That's it. Estimated maxes for isolation exercises aren't meaningful — one validation study found *all nine* standard prediction equations failed on the arm curl.

## 13.5 The rounding problem

Prescribed loads won't land on plates you own. The app should:

1. Calculate the ideal load
2. Round to the nearest achievable increment — 2.5 kg by default, 1 kg if you have micro-plates
3. **Show you the effective RPE after rounding**

So a prescription of 92.4 kg becomes "92.5 kg — effective RPE 8.05." You know exactly what you're doing, and the small deviation is recorded rather than hidden.

You can always override the load manually. The app records the override, computes the resulting RPE, and carries on. Deviations are data, not errors.

## 13.6 "8 reps to failure" — you don't have to predict it

This worry dissolves once you see the structure.

For a prescription like `3×8–12, 1–2 RIR`: you're **not** trying to hit exactly 8. You pick a weight, do reps until you're 1–2 short of failure, and write down what happened. The range is a guide for load selection, not a target.

**Double progression handles the rest automatically:**

- Hit the **top** of the range on every set at the prescribed RIR → add weight next time, drop back to the bottom of the range
- Fall **below** the bottom of the range → the weight was too heavy, reduce it
- Land anywhere **in between** → keep the weight, try for more reps next time

No prediction needed. The app records what happened and suggests the adjustment.

## 13.7 Rest periods

| Work | Rest |
|---|---|
| Heavy singles and doubles (≥90%) | **3–5 min** |
| Heavy back-offs, 3–5 reps | 3–4 min |
| Volume bench, 5s at 75–80% | 2.5–3 min |
| Speed bench | 90–120 s — enough to stay fast |
| Bench variations | 2.5–3 min |
| Weighted pull-ups and dips | 2–3 min |
| Incline press, rows | 2 min |
| Leg press, hack squat | 2–3 min |
| Isolation — laterals, curls, pushdowns, flies | **60–90 s** |
| Leg curls, extensions, calves | 60–90 s |
| Jumps | **Full recovery — 2–3 min.** Never conditioning. |

**Why long on compounds and short on isolation:** with long rests, per-session growth for a muscle plateaus around 6–8 sets; with short rests it extends further but needs roughly double the volume. So spend your long rests where load actually matters, and keep isolation dense — you're chasing failure there anyway, and the load is not the point.

## 13.9 Bar speed and intent — where it applies

**Yes, drive every concentric with maximal intent — on compounds.**

Bench, incline, dips, pull-ups, rows, leg press: **~2 second controlled eccentric, then accelerate as hard as you can.** This is worth roughly double the strength gain versus deliberately slow lifting, from identical loads and volume.

Two things people get wrong:

- **Intent is not the same as speed.** At 92% the bar will move slowly no matter what. Intent is about the attempt, not the result.
- **Intent is not looser form.** The eccentric stays controlled, the pause stays honest, the bar path stays the same. Only the concentric changes.

**Do NOT apply this to isolation work.** Lateral raises, curls, flies, leg curls — controlled tempo throughout. Explosive lateral raises become swinging, which takes tension off the exact muscle you're trying to grow. There, tension is the goal, not force output.

The distinction: **intent when you're training force production, control when you're training a muscle.**

And note the intent effect disappears when sets are taken to failure — at failure, fatigue recruits everything regardless. So on your `[F]` isolation sets, intent is irrelevant anyway. The two rules agree.

## 13.10 Rest days — before or after?

Both are legitimate, for different reasons:

- **Rest AFTER a hard session** removes fatigue that's actually present. Fatigue from heavy or failure work peaks at 24–48 h.
- **Rest BEFORE a hard session** means you go in fresher and perform better on that specific day.

Which you choose depends on what the session is *for*:

| Session | Rest placement | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **A (heavy)** | **After** | The fatigue is real and needs clearing. Going in slightly tired costs you little — the load is autoregulated by RPE anyway. |
| **E (AMRAP)** | **Before** | This is a **measurement**. A tired reading is a corrupted reading, and a corrupted reading means we misjudge your progress. |
| **F (jumps)** | **Before** | Power work is worthless when fatigued. |
| **C (volume)** | Either | Genuinely doesn't matter. |

**The simple version:** rest after A, and rest before E. If you only get one rest day per cycle, put it after A.

**Never rest between C and D.** The speed day is deliberately cheap and partly exists to flush the volume day. Skipping past it wastes the design.

## 13.11 Missing sessions — you're probably overthinking this

With a rolling rotation, **a missed day isn't a decision you have to make.** There's no "which session do I drop." You simply do the next session in the rotation the next time you train. The rotation slides. That's the whole point of not using a calendar week.

- **Train 6 times in 7 days** → you complete a full rotation
- **Train 5 times in 7 days** → the rotation takes about 8 days. Nothing changes. Don't compensate.
- **Train 4 times in 7 days, once** → same. Just keep going.

You only switch templates when you *know in advance* you'll be limited for **two weeks or more** — Christmas, a trip, a heavy work period. Then you move to the 4-day or 3-day version in Part 5, and switch back afterward.

**"Drop Session D" is different advice**, for a different situation: chronic under-recovery. If sleep has been below 7 hours for a week or more, D is the cheapest session to lose because it's the lowest-stimulus one. That's a recovery decision, not a scheduling one.

## 13.12 When a set feels easier than prescribed — the rule

This is the most important operational rule in the plan, and it has two halves depending on how the set was written.

### Fixed reps + an RPE target → **vary the load, not the reps**

Example: `5 reps @ RPE 8`, prescribed at 100 kg. You do 5 and it felt like 4 in reserve.

**Do not do extra reps.** Instead:

1. Record it honestly: *5 reps, RPE 6*
2. **Add load on the next set** — roughly 2.5–5 kg per RPE point you were under
3. The app flags that your working max is probably low

Why not just add reps? Because the prescription is a *strength* stimulus with a defined rep count, and 7 reps at 100 kg is a different exercise from 5 at 100 kg. It also breaks your log's comparability — you can no longer compare this session's 5s to last session's 5s. The whole measurement system depends on holding one variable steady.

### A rep range + an RIR target → **vary the reps, then the load**

Example: `3×8–12, 1–2 RIR`.

The range exists precisely for this. If you reach 8 and still have 4 in reserve, **keep going** — to 10, 12, wherever 1–2 RIR actually lands. If you hit 12 with reps still in the tank, stop at 12 and **add weight next session**. That's double progression, and the range is doing its job.

### `[F]` sets → go until failure, whatever the number

No prescription to violate. Record what happened.

**The one-line version:** *fixed reps means the load moves. A range means the reps move.*

## 13.13 Index sets — weekly estimated-max data without extra fatigue

You suggested a weekly set to failure on every exercise to keep maxes current. Good instinct, but doing it on everything would reintroduce exactly the fatigue problem we designed around — and for isolation exercises an estimated max isn't meaningful anyway (one validation study found all nine standard prediction equations failed on the arm curl).

**The fix: one designated "index set" per key lift, done fresh, once per rotation.** These are already in the plan — they're just being *labelled* so the app knows which set to read.

| Lift | Index set | To failure? |
|---|---|---|
| **Comp bench** | The AMRAP, Session E — **now first in the session** | Yes |
| Comp bench (second reading) | The top single, Session A | No — RPE 8 |
| Incline press | First working set, Session C | No — RPE 8 |
| Weighted pull-up | First working set, Session A | No — RPE 8 |
| Weighted dip | First working set, Session E | No — RPE 8 |
| Leg press | First working set, Session B | No — RPE 8 |

A top set at a known RPE gives a cleaner estimated max than a set to failure, at a fraction of the fatigue cost. Only the bench AMRAP needs to go to failure, because it's the one number where we want zero interpretation.

**"Fresh" means first in the session, after the warm-up ramp.** That's why the AMRAP moved to the top of Session E — the measurement was previously being taken after four sets of variation work, which corrupted it.
