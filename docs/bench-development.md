# Blowing up the bench press

**Written 23 August 2026**, answering: *"what are some of the exercises that
people credit as the best for blowing up the bench press? I heard some people
say that weighted pull ups are the best, some people say that dead stop is
important."*

Both of those claims are real, and **one of them is much more true than the
other**. This document takes them in order, then gives the honest hierarchy.

Nothing here changes your plan. Where the plan already does something, I say so
and give the slot.

---

## The two claims you brought

### "Weighted pull-ups are the best thing for your bench"

**Partly true, for reasons that are not the ones usually given.**

What is genuinely true:

- **The upper back is the surface you press from.** A bench press is a closed
  chain against the bench: the more rigid and the thicker that platform, the
  more force goes into the bar instead of into you sinking into the pad. A
  bigger, tighter upper back is a physically better platform.
- **A thicker back shortens the bar path.** Lying on more muscle raises your
  chest. That is free range-of-motion reduction, and it is measurable.
- **The lats control the descent and set the bar path.** In a competition bench
  the bar travels down and back toward the chest and up and forward to lockout —
  a J. The lats own the "back" half of that. A bench where the bar drifts and
  the shoulders lose position is usually a lat problem, not a pec problem.
- **They keep you benching.** Pressing volume with no pulling volume is how
  shoulders get unhappy, and the fastest way to lose bench is to stop benching.

What is **not** true, and where the claim gets overstated:

- The lats are not a prime mover in the concentric. They do not push the bar up.
- The correlation people cite — strong pull-up guys have strong benches — is
  substantially confounded. People with strong weighted pull-ups train hard,
  have been training for years, and are usually not fat. Those things predict a
  strong bench on their own.
- **A weighted pull-up is not a bench exercise.** It is the best *back* exercise
  you can progressively load, and back size is worth building for its own sake
  on a bulk. Treat it as that, not as bench assistance, and you will not be
  disappointed when your bench does not move the week your pull-up does.

**In your plan:** A5, weighted pull-up (normal grip), 4 × 5–8 at RPE 8, and D5,
weighted chin-up (supinated), 3 × 6–10 at RPE 8. Plus rows at A6, C5, E5, F3.
This is already well covered — arguably better covered than your triceps.

**Verdict: yes, do them, and they are already prescribed. No, they are not the
main thing.**

### "Dead stop is important"

**This one is straightforwardly true, and it is the more useful of the two.**

A normal touch-and-go bench borrows from three things at the bottom:

1. **The stretch-shortening cycle** — elastic energy stored in the eccentric,
   worth real kilos if you reverse quickly.
2. **The rib cage bouncing the bar.**
3. **Momentum**, if you are honest about it.

A dead-stop or paused rep removes all three, which does two things:

- **It trains the position where you are actually weak.** Almost everyone's
  hardest inch is just off the chest. Touch-and-go is the one style that lets
  you skip past it.
- **It removes the place where technique hides.** If your bar path is wrong or
  your shoulders lose position at the bottom, a pause forces you to hold that
  wrong position under load and you find out.

The forms of it, weakest to strongest medicine:

| | What it is | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| **Paused bench** | Touch, hold 1–2 s motionless, press | Everyone. The default. |
| **Spoto press** | Stop 2–3 cm **above** the chest and hold | Keeping tension, no chest contact — brutal, brilliant for control |
| **Pin press / dead stop** | Bar rests dead on pins at or near chest height, press from zero | The purest off-the-chest strength; no elastic contribution at all |
| **Deficit / larsen** | Increases range or removes leg drive | Specific weak points, later |

Two cautions. **Pin presses at chest height are hard on the shoulder** if you
relax completely under the bar between reps — stay tight, keep the shoulders
packed even while the bar is dead. And **a pause is not a rest**: the tension
never leaves, you are just not moving.

**In your plan:** C2 is exactly this — "Bench variation (close-grip / Spoto /
pin press)", 3 × 4–6 at RPE 8, once per rotation, with a dedicated working max
(`benchVar`) tracked separately from the competition bench. A1 is a static hold
against rack pins, which is a close cousin. **So the plan already agrees with
whoever told you this.**

The one thing worth deciding is which of the three C2 options you actually run,
because the plan currently offers all three under one name — that is on the list
of compound names to split. My recommendation, if you want one: **Spoto press
while the loads are moderate, pin press in the later heavy blocks.**

## The honest hierarchy

Ordered by how much a kilo of effort spent here moves your bench. This is where
the arguing usually goes wrong: people compare exercises when the first three
items on the list are not exercises.

### 1. Bodyweight — and you are already doing it
On a bulk this is the cheapest bench progress available, and it is not cheating.
More mass means a thicker torso, a shorter bar path, more leverage into the
bench, and more muscle to press with. The rough historical rule of thumb is
**roughly half to one kilo on the bench for each kilo of bodyweight**, early in
a gaining phase.

### 2. Frequency and specificity
Your plan benches in four of six sessions (A, C, E, F) with four different jobs:
heavy singles and triples, an AMRAP, volume fives, and speed work. That is the
structure that builds a bench. **The bench press is its own best exercise, and
nothing on this list beats doing it more skilfully more often.**

### 3. Technique — worth more than any accessory
Every item below is worth more than any exercise further down this page:

- **Set the shoulder blades first.** Pull them back and *down* into the bench and
  keep them there for the whole set. Not at the start of the rep — for the whole
  set, including while you take the bar out.
- **Take the bar out without losing that.** Have someone hand it off if you can.
  Most lost position happens during the unrack.
- **Arch what you have.** Upper back tight into the bench, chest high, feet
  planted. You do not need a competition arch to benefit from a rigid one.
- **Leg drive.** Push the floor away *horizontally* — you are driving yourself
  into the bench and back into your traps, not lifting your hips.
- **Bar path is a J, not a line.** Down and slightly back toward the lower
  chest; up and slightly forward toward the shoulders.
- **Elbows around 45–75° from the torso**, not flared to 90 and not pinned to
  the ribs.
- **Bend the bar apart.** Trying to snap the bar outward turns the lats and the
  whole shoulder girdle on. Costs nothing, works immediately.
- **Control the eccentric, then be violent.** Two seconds down, maximum intent
  up, on every rep including warm-ups.
- **Breathe and brace at the top**, hold it for the rep.

### 4. Fix your own sticking point
Where the bar stalls tells you what to train. Watch a set from the side, or feel it:

| Stalls | Usually means | Train |
|---|---|---|
| **Off the chest** | Pecs at long length, plus position lost at the bottom | Paused / Spoto / pin press, wide-ish grip, incline work |
| **Mid-range** | Pec and front delt strength | Close-grip bench, volume fives, dips |
| **Lockout** | **Triceps**, almost always | Close-grip, board or pin press high, overhead extensions |

Lockout is the most common and the most fixable.

### 5. The accessory exercises that actually matter

**Close-grip bench press — the single best bench assistance exercise.** Same
groove, more triceps, and it transfers to the competition lift more directly
than anything else on this list. Grip about shoulder width, elbows tucked
slightly more. It is one of the C2 options in your plan.

**Weighted dips — the best "bench-shaped" hypertrophy exercise.** Loads the
pecs and triceps together through a long range, and it loads easily. Lean
forward for chest, stay upright for triceps. **In your plan: C3, 3 × 6–10, with
its own tracked max.** Caution: they are the exercise most likely to annoy a
shoulder — if the bottom pinches, shorten the range, don't push through.

**Overhead triceps extension — the most under-trained muscle in most benches.**
The long head of the triceps crosses the shoulder, so it is only loaded at long
length when the arm is overhead. Pushdowns, skullcrushers and close-grip work
all train the lateral and medial heads far more than the long head. This is
where the biggest cheap gain usually sits.

> **Worth flagging from your own numbers.** In a typical rotation your plan
> gives the triceps **long head about 9.0 sets a week** against **20.5 for the
> lateral and medial heads**. Every set of the long head comes from A8 and E7
> (overhead cable/rope extension). If you ever want one thing added to the
> pressing side of this plan, a second overhead extension slot is the one I
> would argue for — but that is a training-content change and it is your call,
> not mine.

**Incline press (dumbbell or Smith).** Upper chest and front delt, at a slightly
different angle from the competition groove. **In your plan: A4 and E3/E4.**

**Overhead press.** Front delts and lockout stability, plus it keeps the
shoulder strong through a range the bench never visits. **In your plan: F2, the
OHP ladder.**

**Rows and pull-ups.** Item one, above. **In your plan: A5, A6, C5, D5, E5, F3.**

**Rear delts and face pulls.** They do not add kilos. They keep you healthy
enough to keep adding kilos, which amounts to the same thing over 33 rotations.
**In your plan: F5, and D7.**

### 6. The two things nobody trains and everybody should

- **The rotator cuff** — external rotation work, two sets, twice a week, light.
  Ten minutes a month against the injury most likely to end a bench cycle.
- **The serratus anterior** — it holds the shoulder blade against the rib cage,
  which is what "tight upper back" actually depends on mechanically.

> **Also from your plan.** Neither the rotator cuff nor the serratus appears
> anywhere in the muscle model — not as an exercise and not as a muscle the
> volume analytics can count. That is a genuine gap in coverage rather than a
> gap in your training, but it means the app cannot warn you about it. Adding
> either is a training-content change, so I am flagging it, not doing it.

## What I would tell you if you only read one paragraph

Keep benching four times a rotation with the four different jobs the plan
already assigns. Keep gaining weight at the rate the plan says. **Pause or
dead-stop your bench variation** — that advice you heard is correct and C2
already exists to do it. **Keep the weighted pull-ups**, but for your back and
your platform, not because they will move your bench next month. Fix your
sticking point rather than collecting exercises. And if you want one thing
added, make it **a second overhead triceps extension slot** — the long head is
the muscle your plan trains least and your lockout needs most.
