## What you have, and what each one is for

These are the five you named. Taking them as the primary lifts is not a
compromise; four of them are the best available tool for their job regardless
of what else the gym has.

### Leg press — your main quad driver
**Keep as primary.** The plan already says why: *"your main quad driver without
any spinal loading."* For someone whose back is the limiting factor, a machine
that loads the quads hard with the spine supported is not second best, it is
first.

- **Tip that matters most:** stop the descent the instant the pelvis starts to
  tuck. That tuck is loaded lumbar flexion — the exact thing that irritates you.
  A slightly shorter range with a neutral spine beats a deep rep that costs a
  week.
- **Feet low and narrow** biases quads; **high and wide** biases glutes and
  adductors and reduces knee travel. These are different enough to be two
  different exercises, which matters below.
- **One leg at a time** is a genuinely separate exercise on the same machine:
  more range, no way to hide a side difference, and half the systemic cost. Your
  quads differ 55.6 vs 56.2 cm, so there is something to find.

### Hip thrust — your glute and hinge work
**Keep as primary.** The only loaded hip extension you have that does not
compress the spine the way a Romanian deadlift or a good morning does.

- **Tip:** the failure mode is finishing with the lower back rather than the
  glutes. Ribs down, chin tucked, and stop the rep when the hips stop rising —
  not when the back arches further.

### Quad extension — the only isolated knee extension
**Keep as primary.** It is the only thing that trains the rectus femoris at any
length, because on every squat pattern the hip is flexing while the knee extends
and the rectus never shortens.

- **Tip:** at 12–20 reps this is meant to be uncomfortable, not heavy. Load it
  so the last two reps are genuinely hard rather than so the stack moves.

### Seated hamstring curl — better than the lying one you cannot do
**Keep as primary, and stop thinking of it as second best.** The seated curl
holds the hip flexed, which puts the hamstrings at a **longer** muscle length
than the lying version. Training at long lengths is the one exercise-selection
variable with a reasonably consistent hypertrophy advantage.

**You are not missing anything by having no lying curl.** If anything the swap
is in your favour. What the lying curl does slightly better is the knee-flexion
strength curve at short lengths, which is not what you are training for.

### Standing calf raise — gastrocnemius
**Keep as primary.** Knee straight, so the gastrocnemius is at length and does
the work.

- **Tip:** the calf's problem is almost always range, not load. All the way
  down, pause a beat at the bottom, all the way up. Half a stack moved through
  half a range grows nothing.
- Doing them on the leg press with a straight leg is the same exercise with the
  spine unloaded — a good choice on a day your back is grumbling.

## The one real gap: soleus

The seated calf raise is not a variation of the standing one. **Knee bent takes
the gastrocnemius out of it and leaves the soleus**, which is the larger of the
two by cross-section and the one that responds to it. You currently have no
soleus work at all, and no machine for it.

Three ways out, best first:

1. **Improvised seated calf raise.** Sit on a bench, toes on a plate or step,
   a heavy dumbbell or a loaded bar across the knees just behind the kneecap
   with a pad. Crude, entirely effective, and the only real answer.
2. **Single-leg standing calf raise with a soft knee** — 15–20° of knee bend,
   held. Partially unloads the gastrocnemius. Better than nothing, worse than
   the above.
3. **Leave it.** Calves are the muscle with the largest genetic spread and the
   smallest return on effort, and this is a bench-focused bulk. Defensible.

**This is the only thing in your leg training that is genuinely absent rather
than substituted.**

## What about the ones you cannot or should not do

### Bulgarian split squat — aggravates your back
Not surprising and not you doing it wrong. The rear-foot-elevated position
demands a lot of hip extension range on the front side, and most people find
that range by extending the lumbar spine instead. Add the balance cost and the
trunk stays braced under load for a long time.

**Do not persist with it.** The unilateral quad work it offers is available from
a single-leg press with none of that.

### Smith machine squat — hurts less, still hurts
Better because the bar path is fixed and you do not stabilise, worse because the
fixed path forces your joints to the bar rather than the bar to your joints.
For a back that objects to spinal loading, a squat with the load on your back is
still a squat with the load on your back.

**A reasonable thing to retest later, not a thing to grind on now.**

### Hack squat — you have no access
No loss. It is a leg press with a fixed torso angle. You have the leg press.

### Nordic curl — never tried
Worth trying **once**, from curiosity, not from need. It is a very hard exercise:
almost nobody can do a full one at the start, and the standard entry is
eccentric-only, lowering as slowly as possible and catching yourself.

Given you have a seated curl — which trains the hamstrings at a **longer**
length than a Nordic does — this is a nice-to-have. Try it on a day you have
spare energy, not as a prescribed slot.

**One warning:** Nordics are famous for a specific kind of soreness that lasts
three to five days. Do not do them for the first time within four days of
anything you care about.

## What I would change, and what I need to know first

The obstacle is not what to put in D. It is that **B and D would become the same
session** if I simply put your five exercises in both, and the plan separates
them on purpose.

There are two honest ways to separate them with the equipment you have, and
picking between them is your call:

### Option A — separate by emphasis, same machines

B stays as it is. D uses the same five, but differently:

| | B | D |
|---|---|---|
| Quads | Leg press, feet low/narrow, 8–12 @ RPE 9 | **Single-leg press**, 10–15 per side @ RPE 8 |
| Glutes / hinge | Hip thrust, 6–10 @ RPE 8 | **Leg press, feet high and wide**, 15–25 @ RPE 9 |
| Hamstrings | Seated curl, 8–12 @ RPE 10 | Seated curl, 15–20 @ RPE 10 |
| Calves | Standing, 8–15 | Standing or leg-press, 15–25 |

Honest about the equipment, adds unilateral work you currently have none of, and
changes no session's job. The cost is that it looks repetitive on paper — the
variety is in how you do it rather than in what it is called.

### Option B — narrow D's legs, widen its upper body

Cut D to two leg slots (single-leg press, seated curl) and give the space back
to the upper body work D already has — chin-ups, rear delts, biceps, core.

Fewer leg exercises, more of what you seem to enjoy, and it moves D towards
being a pulling day with legs attached rather than a second leg day. The cost is
lower weekly leg volume, which for a bench-focused bulk is arguably correct
anyway.

### Questions I need answered before writing either one

1. **Does the gym have a reverse hyperextension, or a 45° back extension bench?**
   D9 currently prescribes one and I do not know if it exists for you. If your
   back tolerates it, it is a good low-compression hinge.
2. **Any standing or kneeling single-leg hamstring curl?**
3. **Can the leg press be loaded light enough to use one leg at a time?** Some
   sled presses start too heavy for that.
4. **Is your hip thrust a machine or a barbell?** A machine makes a second
   hinge slot realistic; a barbell setup twice a week is a chore.
5. **Adductor and abductor machines?** Common, and they cover something the
   plan currently misses entirely.
6. **What do you actually find fun?** You said D lacked it. That is not a soft
   consideration — the session you look forward to is the session you do at the
   intensity it asks for. Tell me which exercises you enjoy and I will build
   around them rather than around what a textbook would pick.
