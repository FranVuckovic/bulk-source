## What you have, and what each one is for

These are the five you named. Taking them as the primary lifts is not a
compromise; four of them are the best available tool for their job regardless
of what else the gym has.

### Leg press — your main quad driver
**Keep as primary.** The plan already says why: *"your main quad driver without
any spinal loading."* For someone whose back is the limiting factor, a machine
that loads the quads hard with the spine supported is not second best, it is
first.

- **Tip that matters most:** stop the descent the instant the pelvis starts to
  tuck. That tuck is loaded lumbar flexion — the exact thing that irritates you.
  A slightly shorter range with a neutral spine beats a deep rep that costs a
  week.
- **Feet low and narrow** biases quads; **high and wide** biases glutes and
  adductors and reduces knee travel. These are different enough to be two
  different exercises, which matters below.
- **One leg at a time** is a genuinely separate exercise on the same machine:
  more range, no way to hide a side difference, and half the systemic cost. Your
  quads differ 55.6 vs 56.2 cm, so there is something to find.

### Hip thrust — your glute and hinge work
**Keep as primary.** The only loaded hip extension you have that does not
compress the spine the way a Romanian deadlift or a good morning does.

- **Tip:** the failure mode is finishing with the lower back rather than the
  glutes. Ribs down, chin tucked, and stop the rep when the hips stop rising —
  not when the back arches further.

### Quad extension — the only isolated knee extension
**Keep as primary.** It is the only thing that trains the rectus femoris at any
length, because on every squat pattern the hip is flexing while the knee extends
and the rectus never shortens.

- **Tip:** at 12–20 reps this is meant to be uncomfortable, not heavy. Load it
  so the last two reps are genuinely hard rather than so the stack moves.

### Seated hamstring curl — better than the lying one you cannot do
**Keep as primary, and stop thinking of it as second best.** The seated curl
holds the hip flexed, which puts the hamstrings at a **longer** muscle length
than the lying version. Training at long lengths is the one exercise-selection
variable with a reasonably consistent hypertrophy advantage.

**You are not missing anything by having no lying curl.** If anything the swap
is in your favour. What the lying curl does slightly better is the knee-flexion
strength curve at short lengths, which is not what you are training for.

### Standing calf raise — gastrocnemius
**Keep as primary.** Knee straight, so the gastrocnemius is at length and does
the work.

- **Tip:** the calf's problem is almost always range, not load. All the way
  down, pause a beat at the bottom, all the way up. Half a stack moved through
  half a range grows nothing.
- Doing them on the leg press with a straight leg is the same exercise with the
  spine unloaded — a good choice on a day your back is grumbling.

## The one real gap: soleus

The seated calf raise is not a variation of the standing one. **Knee bent takes
the gastrocnemius out of it and leaves the soleus**, which is the larger of the
two by cross-section and the one that responds to it. You currently have no
soleus work at all, and no machine for it.

Three ways out, best first:

1. **Improvised seated calf raise.** Sit on a bench, toes on a plate or step,
   a heavy dumbbell or a loaded bar across the knees just behind the kneecap
   with a pad. Crude, entirely effective, and the only real answer.
2. **Single-leg standing calf raise with a soft knee** — 15–20° of knee bend,
   held. Partially unloads the gastrocnemius. Better than nothing, worse than
   the above.
3. **Leave it.** Calves are the muscle with the largest genetic spread and the
   smallest return on effort, and this is a bench-focused bulk. Defensible.

**This is the only thing in your leg training that is genuinely absent rather
than substituted.**

## What about the ones you cannot or should not do

### Bulgarian split squat — aggravates your back
Not surprising and not you doing it wrong. The rear-foot-elevated position
demands a lot of hip extension range on the front side, and most people find
that range by extending the lumbar spine instead. Add the balance cost and the
trunk stays braced under load for a long time.

**Do not persist with it.** The unilateral quad work it offers is available from
a single-leg press with none of that.

### Smith machine squat — hurts less, still hurts
Better because the bar path is fixed and you do not stabilise, worse because the
fixed path forces your joints to the bar rather than the bar to your joints.
For a back that objects to spinal loading, a squat with the load on your back is
still a squat with the load on your back.

**A reasonable thing to retest later, not a thing to grind on now.**

### Hack squat — you have no access
No loss. It is a leg press with a fixed torso angle. You have the leg press.

### Nordic curl — never tried
Worth trying **once**, from curiosity, not from need. It is a very hard exercise:
almost nobody can do a full one at the start, and the standard entry is
eccentric-only, lowering as slowly as possible and catching yourself.

Given you have a seated curl — which trains the hamstrings at a **longer**
length than a Nordic does — this is a nice-to-have. Try it on a day you have
spare energy, not as a prescribed slot.

**One warning:** Nordics are famous for a specific kind of soreness that lasts
three to five days. Do not do them for the first time within four days of
anything you care about.

## Your leg press question, answered properly

You asked: *deep and slow or heavy? glute or quad focused? one leg or two?
combine it with what?* These are the right questions and they have real answers.

### Deep and slow, or heavy?

**Deep and slow, and it is not close.**

The strongest finding in the last decade of hypertrophy research is that
**training a muscle in a lengthened position matters more than training it
against a big number**. Working at long muscle lengths produces more growth than
the same work at short lengths, and partial reps done *only* in the stretched
part of the range match or beat full-range reps for size. On a leg press, the
stretched position is the bottom.

The practical consequence is uncomfortable: **the load that lets you reach full
clean depth is the correct load, even when a heavier one is available.** A leg
press loaded past what your range tolerates turns into a quarter-rep machine,
and a quarter-rep leg press trains almost nothing.

So: **pick the weight that makes 8–12 full-depth reps an RPE 9, with a 2–3
second lowering phase.** Then add weight as the reps improve — not as a way to
shorten the range.

**Depth is limited by your pelvis, not by the machine.** The instant the pelvis
starts to tuck under, you are loading a flexed lumbar spine, which is the exact
thing that has been hurting you. Stop the descent there. That is your bottom
position, and it will get deeper over months as the hips open up. This is the
single most important sentence in this document for your back.

### Glute or quad focused?

**Both, on different days — the foot position genuinely makes them different
exercises.**

| | Feet **low and narrow** | Feet **high and wide** |
|---|---|---|
| Trains | Quads, hard, at long length | Glutes and adductors, hamstrings a little |
| Knee travel | Long — most knee flexion | Short |
| Hip travel | Short | Long — most hip flexion |
| Knees | More stress; skip if they complain | Kind to knees |
| Feels like | A squat without the bar | A hip thrust you push |

That is a real difference in what grows, not a marketing distinction — so it is
a legitimate way to run the leg press twice a week without repeating yourself.

### One leg or two?

**Both, and one-leg belongs in D specifically.** Single-leg pressing is a
different exercise, not a scaled one:

- **More range at the hip.** Nothing for the other thigh to collide with.
- **No hiding a side difference.** Your quads measure 55.6 vs 56.2 cm. Bilateral
  work lets the strong side quietly do more. One leg at a time does not.
- **Half the systemic cost per set.** No breathless leg-press hangover, which is
  most of why D felt grim.
- **Costs twice the time.** Two sets per "set". Budget for it.

### Combine it with what?

Your instinct to pair it was right, and here is what each pairing actually adds:

**Leg press + quad extension — yes, they are complements, not duplicates.**
The leg press is a hip-and-knee press: the rectus femoris (the quad head that
crosses the hip) never gets to work hard, because it is being lengthened at one
end and shortened at the other at the same time. The quad extension is the only
exercise you have where the knee is the only joint moving, so it is the only one
that trains the rectus femoris properly. **Press first, extension after.**

**Leg press + hamstring curl — necessary, but not a pairing.** The leg press
trains the hamstrings essentially not at all. The hamstrings do two separate
jobs — bending the knee and extending the hip — and they need one exercise for
each:

- *Knee flexion*: seated leg curl. Yours, and the good one. With the hip flexed,
  the hamstrings are loaded at a **long** length, which is why a seated curl
  grows them substantially more than a lying curl. Not having a lying curl is
  not a loss.
- *Hip extension*: the **45° back extension**, which you have. This is the
  slot that has been missing.

**Hip thrust + back extension — these are the pairing you were reaching for
when you said "hip thrusts and reverse hypers".** They are opposites in the best
way:

| | Hip thrust | 45° back extension |
|---|---|---|
| Hardest point | Lockout — glutes at their **shortest** | Bottom — glutes and hamstrings at their **longest** |
| Spinal load | Almost none | Almost none |
| Loads easily? | Yes, machine | Yes, hug a plate |

A muscle trained hard at both ends of its range grows more than one trained at
either. **The back extension is your reverse-hyper replacement and it is a
straight upgrade** — easier to load, easier to control, and the reverse hyper's
swing is mostly momentum anyway.

Two ways to run it, and they are different exercises:
- **Hamstring-biased:** back flat, hinge purely at the hip, legs straight-ish.
  You will feel it entirely in the hamstrings.
- **Glute-biased:** upper back deliberately rounded, chin tucked, and finish by
  squeezing the glutes into a posterior tilt rather than by extending the spine.

For your back, use the **hamstring-biased** version and stop the top of the rep
when your torso is in line with your legs — **not above it**. Going past that
line is lumbar extension under load, which is the mirror image of the mistake
you make at the bottom of the leg press.

**Adductor machine — worth having, and not vanity.** The adductor magnus is one
of the largest muscles in the body and is a genuine hip extensor, working
alongside the glutes. Wide-stance leg pressing trains it already, but direct
work is cheap and it is currently the biggest untrained muscle in your lower
body. The abductor machine (gluteus medius/minimus) is smaller and matters more
for hip stability than size — nice to have, first thing to cut.

### Kneeling cable crunch — how it is actually done
Rope at head height, kneel facing the stack, rope held at your temples or behind
your head.

**The hips do not move.** That is the whole exercise. You are not pulling the
weight down with your arms and you are not bowing at the hip — you are curling
your ribcage toward your pelvis and letting the spine round, then controlling it
back up. Cue: *shorten the distance between your sternum and your belt buckle.*

The near-universal mistake is hinging at the hips, which turns it into a weak,
comfortable lat pulldown and explains why a lot of people think it does nothing.
Pause a beat at the bottom. If you cannot feel it in one or two sets, the weight
is too heavy — this exercise gets worse with load long before it gets better.

### Ab wheel rollout — how it is actually done, and whether to bother
Knees on a pad, wheel under your shoulders. **Before you move at all: pull your
ribs down and squeeze your glutes**, so your pelvis tucks slightly and your
lower back is flat. Hold that, then roll out only as far as you can go *without
the lower back arching*, and pull back with your abs rather than your hips.

For most people starting out, "as far as you can go" is barely past your
shoulders. That is not a failure — the exercise is resisting extension, not
touching your nose to the floor. Two failure modes: the hips pike up and it
becomes a shoulder exercise, or the lower back sags and you feel it in the spine
instead of the abs. Either means roll out less far.

**The easy way in:** roll out toward a wall placed so it stops you at your
current limit, and move the wall back a couple of centimetres every few weeks.

**But given your back, I would not have made this your only anti-extension
exercise, and I would not have made it a slot you have never performed.** It is
the exercise most likely to teach you a bad habit under load. Both proposals
below move it to optional and put a leg raise in its place.

### Leg raises — the one you already know
The version you have done is fine and the version that works is slightly
different:

- **The useful part is the end**, where you tilt the pelvis backwards and curl
  the hips off the floor or off the bar. Lifting straight legs to 90° and
  stopping is almost entirely hip flexor.
- **Hanging** > lying, if you can hold the bar — you already hang for chin-ups.
  If grip gives out before the abs do, that is a reason to use straps, not a
  reason to stop.
- **Regressions in order:** bent-knee hanging raise → straight-leg hanging raise
  → hanging raise with a pelvic tuck at the top. Lying, with your hands tucked
  under your hips, if the bar is occupied.
- **Loading it:** reps first, all the way to 15–20, then a dumbbell between the
  feet or ankle weights.

## Substitutions: the rules, and what to pay attention to

You asked for flexibility. Here is how to swap something without quietly
breaking what the slot was for. The app will offer you a sorted list of similar
exercises; these are the rules that list is trying to encode.

**1. Match the joint action, not the machine.** This is the one that catches
everybody. A back extension does not replace a leg curl — one extends the hip,
the other bends the knee, and the hamstrings do both jobs separately. A hip
thrust does not replace a leg press. Ask *"which joint moves, and in which
direction"* before *"does it look similar"*.

**2. Match the length the muscle is loaded at.** Two exercises for the same
muscle are not interchangeable if one loads it stretched and the other loads it
short. Seated curl (hip flexed, hamstrings long) and lying curl (hip straight,
hamstrings short) train the same joint action and grow the muscle by markedly
different amounts. **When in doubt, take the version that is hardest at the
bottom of the range.**

**3. Watch the spinal cost — this is your rule specifically.** Most machine → free
weight substitutions add spinal load. That is exactly how D ended up prescribing
three things that hurt or were impossible. Before accepting any swap: *does the
load sit on my spine, and does the exercise ask my lower back to hold position
under fatigue?* Two yeses, find something else.

**4. Move the rep range with the exercise, not with the slot.** A bilateral
3 × 8–12 becomes 3 × 10–15 per leg when it goes unilateral, because single-leg
work is more stable and less limited by balance at slightly higher reps. A
bodyweight exercise that replaces a loaded one starts at higher reps too.

**5. If the substitute is easier to load, keep the RPE and let the weight
move. If it is harder to load, add reps first.** A 45° back extension gets a
plate hugged to the chest only once 15 clean bodyweight reps are easy.

**6. The bench slots are not substitutable on preference.** A1, C1, E1, E2 and
F1 exist as a set of related stimuli — competition bench, a variation, volume,
speed. Swapping one because a bench is busy is fine and the app will suggest
sensible alternatives. Swapping one because you would rather do something else
changes what the plan is.

**7. When two options are genuinely equivalent, the app should say so.** That is
the compound-name splitting you asked for — "hip thrust (barbell or machine)"
becomes two exercises with the machine as your default, and the app tells you
they are equivalent rather than making you guess which one it meant.

### What to pay attention to, per muscle

| Muscle | The thing that most affects whether it grows |
|---|---|
| Quads | Depth. Range beats load, every time. |
| Hamstrings | Whether the hip is flexed. Both jobs, both trained. |
| Glutes | Getting the top of the rep from the glutes, not the lower back. |
| Adductors | Stance width, wherever you press from. |
| Calves | A full pause at the bottom, and enough reps. Soleus needs a bent knee. |
| Abs | Loading them. Endless unloaded reps are cardio for the trunk. |
