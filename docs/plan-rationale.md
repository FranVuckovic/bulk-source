# Why this plan is what it is

**The reasoning, the evidence, and the trade-offs behind every decision.**

This is the document to read in eighteen months when you have forgotten why
something is the way it is — or when you are tempted to change it.

---

## Part 1 · What this plan is for

Two goals, in this order of difficulty:

1. **Gain about 10 kg, most of it lean**, with the aesthetic muscles —
   upper chest, side delts, arms — carrying a disproportionate share.
2. **Bench as much as possible**, with a peaked 140 kg by the end of March.

They are not in conflict. Size is what eventually moves the bench, and a bigger
chest, triceps and delts are both the aesthetic goal and the strength mechanism.
Where they *do* conflict — heavy low-rep work is inefficient for growth, and
high-rep failure work is unnecessary for strength — the plan resolves it by
giving each exercise **one job** rather than asking one exercise to do both.
That is the core idea of powerbuilding, and it is why this plan looks the way it
does.

The third goal is unstated but decides the other two: **finish the 33 weeks.**
Your history is short bursts of progress separated by long plateaus in a
deficit. You have never run a structured programme in a sustained surplus. That
is not a small missing variable — it is the whole thing.

## Part 2 · The evidence this is built on

Five findings do most of the work. Where they conflict with common gym wisdom,
the plan follows the evidence.

### Volume drives size, with diminishing returns and no clear ceiling

The largest meta-regression to date (67 studies, 2,058 subjects) found muscle
growth still increasing at the top of the volume range studied. Strength, by
contrast, shows **much sharper** diminishing returns.

**What follows:** more sets keep buying growth, so the limit is not stimulus but
recovery, joints and session length. Put volume where it is cheap — cables,
machines, isolation — and keep it lean where it is expensive. Strength does not
need much volume at all.

The same paper validated **fractional counting**: scoring indirect work at 0.5
predicted growth better than counting only direct sets. That is exactly how this
plan and the app count.

### Closeness to failure drives size, but not strength

A meta-regression on proximity to failure found hypertrophy increasing as sets
end closer to failure — and a **negligible** relationship with strength gain.

**What follows, and this is the most useful single fact in the document:**
because RIR does not matter for strength, your heavy bench work loses nothing by
stopping at RPE 8. And because it does matter for size, everything that is *not*
the bench should finish at or near failure. Failure is aimed, not cut. It goes
on rows, face pulls, laterals, curls, extensions, flies, leg work. It never goes
on heavy pressing.

### Frequency drives strength, not size

The same dose-response work found frequency reliably positive for strength
(100% posterior probability) and essentially irrelevant for hypertrophy once
weekly volume is equated. For the bench specifically, each added session up to
about four is worth roughly **28% faster strength gain** at matched volume, and
10+ weekly bench sets beats fewer than 5 by about 20%.

**What follows:** four bench exposures a week, with only one true-failure set
among them. Frequency is bought with submaximal work, which is exactly what the
Eastern-bloc bench programmes do — three to four sessions a week, mostly at
70–85%, on the principle of *build and practise, do not test*.

### Per-session volume has a ceiling that weekly volume does not

Returns become undetectable past roughly **11 fractional sets for one muscle in
one session**. No session in this plan exceeds that. It is why the work is
spread across four to five exposures rather than piled into two.

### Long muscle lengths grow muscle better than short ones

Training at long lengths produces meaningfully more growth than training short,
though the advantage narrows in trained lifters.

**What follows:** cable laterals with the cable *behind* the body, incline curls
with the elbows *behind* the torso, flies with the arms travelling behind at the
bottom, overhead extensions for the long head, incline dumbbells for the stretch
at the bottom. Every one of those choices is the same choice.

## Part 3 · The structure, decision by decision

### Why a six-session rotation and not a weekly split

The rotation does not care what day it is. You train when you can and always do
the next session. A missed day is not a decision — the rotation simply slides.
This removes the single most common failure mode of week-based programmes: one
disrupted week cascading into an abandoned programme.

Blocks advance on **sessions completed**, not on dates, because a block is an
amount of training, not an amount of time. The calendar is tracked alongside
purely as a pace check, because the March target is a real date.

### Why the sessions run in this order

A controlled trial found a statistically significant bench advantage for the
order that puts the *low-fatigue* session between the volume session and the
heavy session. So the volume day (C) is followed by the speed day (D), not by
the AMRAP, and the heavy day (A) always sits two sessions after the AMRAP —
following F, the leg day.

**Never reorder so that A directly follows C or E.**

### Why four bench exposures with only one to failure

Fatigue tracks how close you go to failure, not how many sets you do. Six sets
at 3 RIR recover as fast as three sets at 3 RIR; three sets to failure cost
24–48 hours. That is the entire reason you can bench four times a week.

The four exposures each have one job:

| | Job |
|---|---|
| **A · heavy single + back-offs** | Max-strength specificity and skill at competition load |
| **C · primer single + 5×5** | The main hypertrophy driver, plus a second heavy exposure |
| **D · speed bench 6×3 @ RPE 6** | Skill practice and intent, at almost no fatigue cost |
| **E · AMRAP** | The weekly measurement — and the only true-failure press |

### Why the single alone is not the training

A controlled trial ran heavy-single-alone against heavy-single-plus-back-offs
for six weeks. Single alone: +11.4 kg, with a 6.3% probability of a meaningful
change. Single plus 2×3 at 80% of it: **+33.7 kg, 99.6% probability.**

The single is a load-finder. The back-offs are the training. Skipping them turns
session A into the thing that failed you before.

### Why the speed day counts as zero volume

Sets at RPE 5–6 are motor-pattern practice, not growth stimulus. Counting them
would inflate the ledger and — worse — tempt you to make the day harder, which
is the most common way people wreck a heavy/light structure.

The reason it exists at all: with volume and load equated across six weeks,
varying only the *intent* to move the bar fast produced **+18.2% on the bench**
against +9.7% for deliberately slow lifting. Double the gain from identical
work. The effect only exists when sets are not taken to failure, which is
another reason failure stays off the bench.

### Why the AMRAP is first in session E

It is a measurement, and a tired reading is a corrupted reading. It used to sit
after four sets of variation work, which corrupted it. Load stays in the 80–87%
window because there the rep count agrees between sessions to within about one
rep and formula error at ≤5 reps is near zero. At 70% the between-person spread
runs from 6 to 28 reps and the error roughly doubles.

Never more than one per week: a compound set to failure costs 24–48 hours, and
two per week means each measurement is taken against incomplete recovery from
the last.

### Why exercises rotate on blocks and never weekly

A systematic review found excessive rotation may actively hinder adaptations,
and a direct trial comparing fixed exercises against randomly varied ones found
the fixed group gained **+4.7% on bench** against **+0.77%** for the varied
group. The rule: anchor what you measure, rotate what you don't.

### Why the working max is frozen between blocks

**This is the most important mechanism in the plan.**

Test–retest variability on a 1RM in trained lifters is about 3.3%, which on a
130 kg bench is ±4 kg of pure noise. If the working max ratcheted up on every
good session, six weeks later you would be training at a genuine RPE 9.5 while
believing you were at 8. That is overreaching by arithmetic, and it is almost
impossible to detect from the inside.

So there are two numbers, always:

- **Observed e1RM** — computed from index sets, a continuous history, never overwritten.
- **Working max** — what prescriptions are calculated from, changing only at block boundaries, and only with an explicit confirmation.

Mid-block it rises only if observed beats it by more than 5% for two consecutive
weeks. It never falls mid-block — a bad week is a bad week, not a strength loss.
At a boundary it may fall, by at most 5%, and the app says so loudly.

### Why only seven lifts have a stored max

Estimated maxes on isolation work are not meaningful: one validation study found
*all nine* standard prediction equations failed on the arm curl. Every exercise
can carry a max for prescription purposes, but each is tagged `high` or `ind`,
and **indicative maxes never appear in a chart or a PR claim**.

### Why index sets exist

One designated set per key lift, done fresh, once per rotation. A top set at a
known RPE gives a cleaner estimated max than a set to failure at a fraction of
the fatigue cost — only the bench AMRAP goes to failure, because it is the one
number where we want zero interpretation.

Sets above 12 reps produce no e1RM at all. Past there the estimate stops being
meaningful, and a number that looks precise and is not is worse than no number.

### Why bodyweight is part of the load on pull-ups and dips

A +25 kg pull-up at 90 kg bodyweight is a 115 kg lift. Treating it as a 25 kg
lift makes every percentage, RPE and e1RM on that exercise meaningless. The app
computes on the total and displays the added weight, because the added weight is
what you put on the belt. Each set stores the bodyweight it was computed
against, so an old pull-up log stays interpretable when you weigh 96 kg.

### Why deloads are earned rather than scheduled

See `plan-changes.md` §11. Briefly: planned deloads have been found to slightly
reduce strength gains against training through, complete cessation is worse
still, and the risk of non-functional overreaching is low. The app watches your
own triggers instead and offers one when two fire together.

## Part 4 · Nutrition, in one page

You do not track calories and you have a large appetite, so this is built around
outcomes rather than arithmetic.

| | Target |
|---|---|
| Weeks 1–12 | +0.4–0.5 kg/week |
| Weeks 13–33 | +0.2–0.25 kg/week |
| Protein | 1.6–2.2 g/kg → **150–200 g/day** |
| Creatine | 3–5 g/day, monohydrate, no loading |

Faster early because muscle memory converts food into muscle at a better ratio
in the first 8–12 weeks; throttle back once you are past your old peak.

**Two instruments and one rule.** Scale every morning, after the toilet, before
food — only the 7-day average counts. Tape at the navel once a week, relaxed,
same time of day. **Adjust on 2–3 week trends, never on single readings.**

The waist number is what separates a lean bulk from a fat one. Weight up, waist
nearly flat, lifts up is what it is supposed to look like. If the waist is
rising at more than roughly a third of the rate bodyweight is, the surplus is
too big regardless of what the scale says.

**The one thing worth counting is protein** — it is the only nutrient where you
cannot see the deficit until months of training have been wasted.

## Part 5 · What could still go wrong

Ranked by probability × cost.

1. **Not finishing.** Five to six sessions a week at 70–95 minutes. Everything
   else on this list is smaller than this one.
2. **Elbows.** Four pressing days, ~29 whole-muscle triceps sets, dips, the
   overhead ladder, and static holds from block 2. This is the joint every
   change in revision 2 pushed on. Two niggles in a block means rotate the
   aggravating variation out — obey it.
3. **The AMRAP as a single point of failure.** One weekly reading on the
   competition lift, needing a spotter, corrupted by bad sleep or basketball the
   day before. Session A's top single is a partial backstop.
4. **Gain rate running hot.** The waist measurement is the only guard.
5. **Shoulder on the overhead ladder.** Four weeks minimum per rung, advance
   only if the previous rung was completely symptom-free, drop back a rung at
   the first dull ache. No negotiating.

## Part 6 · Honest expectations

| Milestone | Estimate |
|---|---|
| Peaked 140 kg by end of March | 70–80% likely |
| Normal-day 140 kg by end of March | 40–50% |
| 150 kg in any form by March | Unlikely — plausible within 12–18 months |

The most probable outcome is a normal-day bench around 130–135 with a peaked max
of 140–145, at roughly 95–98 kg bodyweight, with visibly bigger delts, arms and
upper chest.

**Finishing 33 weeks of this matters more than any single decision inside it.**
